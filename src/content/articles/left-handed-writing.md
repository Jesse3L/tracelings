Left handed writing goes best when the setup does the work: tilt the paper so the top corner points toward the right shoulder, hold the pencil a little higher up than usual, and seat your child so their elbow has room. Most smudging and "backward" letters come from the way the hand meets the page, not from anything your child is doing wrong. Never try to switch a left-handed child to the right hand.

Below you'll find a simple setup, ideas for grip and smudging, the letters that tend to feel odd, and a short practice routine.

## Let your child use the hand they prefer

Many kids show a clear hand preference somewhere between 3 and 5, and some take longer. Some switch back and forth during the preschool years. That is common.

If your child reaches for the left hand most of the time, let them. Moving a pencil to the other hand does not help and can make writing feel frustrating. If you have questions about hand preference, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

A good way to see which hand your child prefers is to watch what they do without being asked. Which hand picks up the crayon, the spoon, the toothbrush? Offer things in the middle, not placed to one side.

## Set the paper at a slant

For a left-handed writer, a straight page often makes the hand cover the words as they appear. Tilting the paper helps the hand stay below the line.

- **Tilt the page** so the top right corner points a little up and away. Many left-handed writers tilt the top of the paper toward the right shoulder.
- **Slide the page to the left** of center so the writing hand has space to move naturally across it.
- **Tape the corner down** at first so the page doesn't slide while your child gets used to the angle.
- **Try a few angles.** There is no single right number of degrees. The best slant is the one where your child can see what they just wrote.

The paper angle is easy to change, so test a few before you change anything else.

## Check the pencil grip

A left-handed child often pulls the pencil across the page instead of pushing it. That makes it easy to cover the line. Grip height makes a big difference.

Many left-handed writers like to hold the pencil about an inch and a half back from the point, rather than right at the tip. That lets the hand sit lower and out of the way.

A wrapped rubber band or a small sticker on the pencil can mark the spot. Keep it relaxed. If you'd like more detail on how the fingers rest on the pencil, see our guide to the [tripod grasp](/learn/tripod-grasp/).

Some left-handed kids curl the wrist into a hook shape so they can see their writing. A slanted page and a higher grip often help the wrist stay flatter. If a grip looks painful or your child tires very quickly, an occupational therapist can take a closer look.

## Seating and space matter more than you'd think

Left-handed children often bump elbows with a right-handed neighbor. A small change fixes a lot.

- **Seat your child on the left side** of a shared table or bench, so their writing arm has open space.
- **Put the light on the right** so the hand doesn't cast a shadow over the page.
- **Choose a chair height** where the elbow rests a little below the table edge and the feet touch the floor or a stool.
- **Keep the workspace clear** of cups and toys on the left, where the arm travels.

In a classroom, the same ideas apply. If your child keeps bumping a neighbor, a quick word with the teacher about seating may help.

## How to cut down on smudging

Smudging is the most common complaint, and it is mostly about where the hand travels.

1. **Slant the page** first. That alone cuts a lot of smears.
2. **Use a cover sheet.** Slide a blank piece of paper under the writing hand so it rests on clean paper.
3. **Try a different tool.** Some kids smudge less with a harder pencil, a gel pen or a fine crayon. Others prefer a dry-erase marker on a laminated page, which wipes clean.
4. **Let your child write from the top down** and wait a moment before dragging the hand across fresh lines.

A little smudging is normal and not a sign of poor writing. Try not to focus on neatness while your child is still learning how letters are built.

## Letters that can feel backward

Right-handed writers push the pencil away from the body on crossbars, such as the line on a t or an e. Left-handed writers often pull the pencil toward the body, so a horizontal line may feel more natural when it goes from right to left.

You may notice your child:

- **Crossing a t from right to left.** That is common and fine if the letter looks clear.
- **Starting a round letter clockwise** (a, d or g) instead of counterclockwise. Some kids do this whichever hand they use.
- **Turning a letter around** now and then. Reversals are common in early writers, whatever hand they use.

Give your child a model to copy, and let the strokes get readable before worrying about the exact direction. If reversals continue well into the school years, or you have other concerns, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician. Practice with a [letter tracing page for t](/letter-tracing/t/) can show the order of strokes in a way that's easy to follow. You can also start with the whole set of [free letter tracing worksheets](/letter-tracing/) and let your child pick one.

Name writing is another good place to start, since a child's own name feels meaningful. A [name tracing worksheet](/name-tracing/) gives them a clear model with arrows.

## Five-minute routine to try this week

You only need a pencil, a sheet of paper, tape and a cover sheet.

1. **Set the seat (1 minute).** Sit your child on the left side of the table with their feet supported and light coming from the right.
2. **Tilt and tape the page (1 minute).** Angle the paper so the top points toward the right shoulder, then tape one corner down.
3. **Mark the grip (30 seconds).** Put a small sticker an inch and a half from the pencil tip.
4. **Trace one letter or the first letter of their name (2 minutes).** Let your child go slowly, with the hand below the line.
5. **Cheer for the effort (30 seconds).** Point to one letter that looks clear and one habit that worked, like keeping the hand low.

Repeat for a few days. If the angle still feels wrong, try a different tilt before trying a different pencil.
