To homeschool preschool, pick a few small goals (letters, numbers, writing their name), set aside about 10 to 15 minutes of table work a day, and fill the rest with reading aloud and play. You don't need a classroom, a schedule by the hour or expensive supplies. Most of homeschooling preschool happens in short, regular moments with a grown-up nearby.

Below is what to cover between ages 3 and 5, a sample day and week, a short supply list, how to check your state's rules, and a first week you can start on Monday.

## What to cover when you homeschool preschool

Preschool at home is mostly about getting ready to read, write and count. A little of each, most days, goes a long way.

- **Letters and sounds.** Recognizing letters and hearing the sound each one makes. Many families teach one letter a week.
- **Writing their name.** A child's own name is often the first word they want to write. It's also the most useful one.
- **Counting and numbers.** Counting small groups of things, touching each one once, and matching a number to a group.
- **Fine motor skills.** Coloring, cutting with safety scissors, playdough and tracing all build the hand strength writing needs.
- **Read-aloud time.** Picture books every day. This builds vocabulary and listening, and no worksheet replaces it.
- **Play and the world around them.** Blocks, pretend play, nature walks and kitchen helping cover science, shapes and talking skills without any formal lessons.

A simple order helps. Many phonics programs start with letters like s, a, t, i, p and n because those few sounds already make real words (sat, pin, tap). Our [free letter tracing worksheets](/letter-tracing/) cover every letter, capital and lowercase, so you can follow any order you like.

## When to start homeschooling preschool

There is no set start date. Many families begin somewhere between 3 and 4, often in the fall to match the school year, but you can start any week.

A child may be ready for a few minutes of table work when they like to color or scribble, can sit through a short picture book, and notice letters on signs or cereal boxes. If your child isn't there yet, keep reading aloud and playing. Those are the foundation for everything that comes later.

## A sample homeschooling preschool schedule

You don't need a timetable. A short routine at the same time each day, often after breakfast, works well for many families.

A day might look like this:

- **2 minutes:** say the letter of the week and its sound, then name three things that start with it.
- **10 minutes:** one printed page together, like tracing the letter or counting and coloring.
- **3 minutes:** one hands-on idea away from the paper, like a sound hunt around the house or a letter drawn in salt.
- **Later in the day:** a picture book, outdoor play and helping with real jobs, like setting the table and counting forks.

A week can follow a simple rhythm:

- **Monday:** meet the new letter and trace it.
- **Tuesday:** find and circle the letter on a page or in a book.
- **Wednesday:** color a big letter and say words that start with its sound.
- **Thursday:** math day, with a number of the week to count and trace. Free [pre-K math worksheets](/math-worksheets/pre-k/) make counting, ten frame and find the number pages for numbers 1 to 20.
- **Friday:** name practice and a favorite page from the week.

Four days a week is plenty if that fits your family better. Skip a day when someone is sick or tired, and pick it back up the next day. ## What you need to homeschool preschool

The list is short:

- A printer and plain paper. Black and white is fine.
- Crayons or chunky colored pencils, plus a regular pencil for tracing.
- A folder or binder to keep each week's pages together.
- Ten small things to count, like blocks, buttons or crackers.
- A library card, so you have a fresh stack of picture books every week or two.

You can skip a lot of what catalogs show. Preschoolers don't need tests, grades, long worksheet packets or a dedicated school room. A spot at the kitchen table works.

## How to check your state's homeschool rules

Homeschool laws vary a lot by state, and some apply only once a child reaches the age when school attendance is required. Preschool often comes before that age, but don't assume. Your state education department's website or a statewide homeschool group can tell you what applies where you live.

It also helps to keep simple records from the start. A folder of finished pages and a short note of what you covered each week is easy to keep, and it shows progress you might otherwise forget.

## How to know if it's working

You'll see it in small moments: your child points out the letter S on a stop sign, counts the stairs out loud, or asks you to spell their name. That's preschool learning doing its job.

Kids learn at very different speeds. Some kids know most letters by 4, and many are still learning them at 5. Both are common. Writing their name usually comes after tracing it for a while. Slow down if a page brings tears, and move faster if your child is bored. If you have concerns about your child's development, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

## Your first week, step by step

Here's a plan you can start Monday with a printer and a box of crayons.

1. **Sunday (10 minutes):** print a [name tracing worksheet](/name-tracing/) with your child's name and a [letter S tracing page](/letter-tracing/s/). Put them in a folder with a few crayons.
2. **Monday (10 minutes):** trace the first row of their name together, your finger first, then theirs, then a pencil or crayon.
3. **Tuesday (10 minutes):** say the /s/ sound and hunt for three things in the house that start with it. Trace two rows of S.
4. **Wednesday (10 minutes):** count out 5 crackers, touching each one, then eat 2 and count what's left.
5. **Thursday (10 minutes):** print a pre-K counting page for the number 1 or 2 and do it together.
6. **Friday (10 minutes):** trace their name again and compare it with Monday's page. Show them what changed.
