For handwriting, many teachers and occupational therapists start with uppercase letters because they are easier to form and easier to tell apart. For reading, lowercase letters matter more, since almost all the text kids see is lowercase. In practice, most families do both: teach capitals first for early writing, then shift to lowercase while showing both forms side by side.

There is no single right answer, and you will find strong opinions on both sides. Here is how each approach works and how to choose for your child.

## The case for uppercase letters first

Capital letters are built from simple strokes. Lines, circles, and diagonals. They all start at the top and sit on the same line. There are no tails that dip below the line and no tall and short parts to sort out.

That makes them a good fit for 3 and 4 year olds whose hands are still getting stronger. Some reasons people start here:

- **Fewer tricky shapes.** Most capitals are made of straight lines and big curves.
- **Same size.** Every capital is the same height, so kids don't have to think about tall and short letters.
- **Fewer mix-ups.** Lowercase b, d, p, and q are mirror images of each other. Capitals B, D, P, and Q look different, so there is less confusion early on.
- **Kids already see them.** Toys, signs, and alphabet puzzles often use capitals.

Several popular handwriting programs used in schools teach capitals first for these reasons.

## The case for lowercase letters first

Lowercase letters make up most of what we read. Open any picture book and you will see a page full of lowercase with a capital here and there.

Some reasons people start with lowercase:

- **Reading matters most.** Kids who know lowercase letters can recognize real words sooner.
- **Less to unlearn.** A child who writes everything in capitals has to break that habit later.
- **School expectations.** Kindergarten teachers usually want names and words written with a capital first letter and lowercase after.

Montessori classrooms often start with lowercase letters, usually paired with letter sounds.

## What most families end up doing

The two approaches aren't as far apart as they sound. A balanced plan looks like this:

1. **Show both forms from the start.** When you point to the letter A, show "A" and "a" together. Reading both is easier than writing both.
2. **Start writing with capitals if your child is young.** For a 3 or 4 year old, capitals are a gentler first step for the hand.
3. **Teach lowercase writing soon after.** Once your child can make most capitals, start on lowercase, beginning with letters that look like their capitals (c, o, s, v, w, x, z).
4. **Write names the standard way.** Capital first letter, the rest lowercase. "Leo," not "LEO."

If your child is closer to 5 or about to start kindergarten, you can move into lowercase sooner.

## Start your child's name the right way

Names are the exception to the "capitals first" idea. Your child will write their name more than any other word, so it is worth teaching it in the standard form from day one.

## Group letters by how they are formed

Instead of going A to Z, many teachers group letters by stroke. It makes practice feel more connected and helps kids notice patterns.

For capitals, a common order is:

- **Straight lines:** L, F, E, H, T, I
- **Diagonals:** V, W, X, Y, Z, A, K, M, N
- **Curves:** C, O, Q, G, S, U, J, D, P, B, R

For lowercase, a common order groups letters that start the same way:

- **Magic c letters:** c, a, d, g, o, q (all start with a "c" curve)
- **Tall line letters:** l, t, i, k, b, h
- **Hump letters:** n, m, r, h, p
- **Diagonals:** v, w, x, y, z
- **Odd ones:** e, f, j, s, u

You can practice any group with [letter tracing worksheets](/letter-tracing/), one letter at a time, before mixing them together.

## Common mix-ups and how to handle them

Expect backward letters. It is very common for kids to reverse letters like b, d, s, and j, and even numbers like 3 and 7. Many kids keep doing it on and off until around age 7.

What helps:

- **Teach starting points.** Most letters start at the top. Lowercase b starts with a tall line, then the bump. Lowercase d starts with a "c" curve, then the tall line.
- **Don't teach b and d on the same day.** Space them a week or two apart.
- **Use a reference strip.** Tape an alphabet strip with both forms to the table.

If reversals still happen a lot past first grade, or you have other concerns about your child's writing or reading, talk to their teacher. They can tell you how your child compares to classmates and whether it makes sense to look into it further with your pediatrician.

## Numbers follow the same logic

Numbers use the same lines and curves as capitals, so they fit well alongside capital letters in early practice. Adding a bit of [number tracing](/number-tracing/) also gives kids a break from letters without losing the pencil practice.

## A simple 10-minute routine

Here is a routine for a 4 year old who is learning both forms:

1. **1 minute: say both forms.** Pick one letter for the day. Show the capital and lowercase on a card and say the sound.
2. **3 minutes: trace the capital.** Two or three lines, starting at the top each time.
3. **3 minutes: trace the lowercase.** Same letter, lowercase. Talk about how it is the same or different.
4. **2 minutes: find it.** Look for both forms in a picture book. Count how many lowercase ones you find versus capitals.
5. **1 minute: use it.** Write a short word or your child's name that includes the letter.

Rotate through letter groups over a few weeks. Your child will build easy capitals for early writing and get lots of practice with the lowercase letters they will read every day.
