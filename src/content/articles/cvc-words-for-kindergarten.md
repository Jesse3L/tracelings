CVC words for kindergarten are short, three-letter words made of a consonant, a short vowel and a consonant, like cat, bed, pig, dog and sun. Each letter makes just one sound, so they are usually the first words a child can sound out and read on their own. Many kids work on them in kindergarten, starting with short a and adding one vowel at a time.

Below you'll find CVC word lists sorted by vowel, a simple order for teaching them, a few blending games, and a short routine that ties reading to writing.

## What makes a word a CVC word?

CVC stands for consonant, vowel, consonant. The vowel in the middle makes its short sound: the a in cat, the e in bed, the i in pig, the o in dog and the u in sun.

That's what makes these words friendly for new readers. A child says each sound, /c/ /a/ /t/, then pushes the sounds together into "cat." This is called blending, and CVC words are where most kids first practice it.

A few three-letter words look like CVC words but don't behave like them. "Was," "the" and "who" don't follow the usual sounds, so they are taught as sight words instead. If you want to know how those fit in, see [what are sight words](/learn/what-are-sight-words/).

## When do kids start reading CVC words?

Many kids start blending CVC words somewhere between ages 4 and 6, often during kindergarten. Before that, they usually need to know most letter sounds and be able to hear the separate sounds in a spoken word.

Some kids click with blending in a week. Others take a few months, and both are normal. If your child knows their letter sounds but blending still isn't coming after a lot of patient practice, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

## CVC words for kindergarten, sorted by short vowel

These lists stick to letters that make their most common sound. Print one row, or write a few words on index cards.

- **Short a:** cat, hat, mat, sat, bat, can, fan, man, pan, van, cap, map, nap, tap, bag, tag, wag, jam, ham, dad, sad
- **Short e:** bed, red, fed, hen, pen, ten, men, net, pet, wet, jet, leg, peg, beg, web, yes
- **Short i:** pig, big, dig, wig, fig, sit, hit, bit, pin, win, tin, fin, lip, hip, zip, dip, lid, kid, rib
- **Short o:** dog, log, fog, hog, hot, pot, dot, cot, top, mop, hop, pop, job, rod, box, fox
- **Short u:** bug, mug, hug, rug, jug, sun, bun, run, fun, cup, pup, nut, cut, hut, bus, tub, cub

You'll notice many words share an ending, like cat, hat and mat. These groups are called word families. They make a great first step, because only the first sound changes. Our [word family worksheets](/word-families/) have a page for -at, -an, -ig, -op, -ug and more.

## A simple order for teaching CVC words

You don't need a fancy plan. This order works for a lot of families and classrooms:

1. **Short a first.** Words like cat, map and bag are easy to picture and draw.
2. **Then short o.** The o in dog sounds very different from a, so kids rarely mix them up.
3. **Then short i.** Space it out from short e, since the two sound alike to many young ears.
4. **Then short u.** Bug, sun and cup are fun words to act out.
5. **Short e last.** It's often the trickiest vowel to hear, so give it extra time.
6. **Mixed review.** Once each vowel feels comfortable, shuffle words from every list together.

Stay on a vowel for about a week, or longer if it needs it. Mixed practice is where kids really learn to look at the middle letter instead of guessing.

## Easy blending games for CVC words

Short, playful practice beats long sessions. Most of these take five minutes and very little prep.

- **Robot talk.** Say a word in slow robot sounds, "/p/ /i/ /g/," and let your child blend it back into a normal word. This is all listening, with no letters yet.
- **Sound slide.** Write a word on paper with a dot under each letter. Your child touches each dot and says the sound, then slides a finger under the whole word and reads it fast.
- **Change one letter.** Build "cat" with magnet letters. Swap the c for an h, then the a for an i. Each swap makes a new word to read: cat, hat, hit, hip.
- **Picture match.** Draw or print small pictures of a cup, a pig and a hat. Your child reads word cards and lays each one on its picture.
- **Word hunt.** Hide five word cards around the room. When your child finds one, they read it before grabbing the next.
- **Silly or real.** Mix real words with made-up ones like "zat" or "mog." Your child reads each one and decides if it's a real word. Sounding out nonsense words shows they are blending, not memorizing.

## Writing CVC words after tracing each letter

Reading and writing CVC words help each other. When a child writes "sun," they have to listen for each sound and pick the letter that matches it.

Start with the letters themselves. If a letter like b or d keeps getting flipped, a few minutes on a [letter tracing worksheet](/letter-tracing/) before writing words can help. Reversals are common in early writers and usually fade with practice.

Next, trace the whole word. Type a short CVC list into our free [word tracing worksheet maker](/word-tracing/) and you'll get dotted words on handwriting lines. A good pattern is to trace the word once, then write it once on the line below without dots.

Finally, try dictation. Say a word, like "map," and have your child say the sounds out loud and write each letter. Don't worry about perfect letter formation here. The goal is hearing and matching sounds.

Once CVC words feel easy, many kids are ready for a few common sight words alongside them. The [kindergarten sight words list](/sight-words/kindergarten/) pairs well with CVC practice, so you can build tiny sentences like "I see a big dog."

## Common sticking points

A few things trip up a lot of new readers. Each one is easy to work on at home.

- **Adding extra sounds.** Saying "cuh-a-tuh" makes blending harder. Model short, clipped sounds, and stretch the ones that can be held, like /mmm/ and /sss/.
- **Guessing from the first letter.** If "cat" becomes "car," point to each letter and sound it out together.
- **Mixing up short e and i.** Say "bed" and "bid" slowly while your child watches your mouth.
- **Getting tired.** If reading turns into a battle, stop and come back tomorrow. Two good minutes beat ten frustrated ones.

## A 10-minute CVC routine for after school

This routine fits on a kitchen table and needs only paper, a pencil and a few index cards.

1. **Warm up (2 minutes).** Play robot talk with three spoken words from today's vowel.
2. **Read (3 minutes).** Lay out five word cards from one list. Your child uses the sound slide to read each one.
3. **Change a letter (2 minutes).** Build one word with magnet letters and make three new words by swapping one letter at a time.
4. **Trace and write (2 minutes).** Trace two of today's words on a printed sheet, then write each one once without dots.
5. **Finish with a win (1 minute).** End on the easiest word of the day, and let your child read it to someone else in the house.

Run it four days a week with the same vowel. On the fifth day, shuffle cards from every vowel you've covered so far and see what sticks.
