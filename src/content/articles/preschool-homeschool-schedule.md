A good preschool homeschool schedule is short and repeats every day: about 10 to 15 minutes of table work, a read-aloud, time outside and plenty of free play, in the same order most days. You don't need a timetable by the hour. Anchor the learning to something that already happens, like breakfast, and let the rest of the day follow.

Below is a sample day, a sample week, how the schedule changes from age 3 to 5, and a free printable to put it on the fridge.

## What goes in a preschool homeschool schedule

Think in blocks, not subjects. Most days have room for:

- **Table time (10 to 15 minutes):** one printed page or short activity with letters, numbers or their name.
- **Read-aloud (10 to 20 minutes, spread out):** picture books, at any time of day.
- **Outside time:** as long as weather and your day allow. Running, climbing and digging build the big muscles that help with sitting and writing later.
- **Hands-on play:** blocks, playdough, puzzles, pretend play, drawing.
- **Rest or quiet time:** a nap, or 30 to 60 minutes of books and quiet toys for a child who has dropped naps.
- **Meals, snacks and real jobs:** setting the table, sorting socks and stirring a recipe count as learning too.

If you're still deciding what to teach, our guide on [how to homeschool preschool](/learn/how-to-homeschool-preschool/) covers what to cover between 3 and 5. This guide is about fitting it into the day.

## A sample preschool homeschool schedule for one day

Here is a morning-focused day. Many preschoolers are freshest early, so the table work comes right after breakfast.

- **7:00:** wake up, get dressed.
- **7:30:** breakfast, brush teeth.
- **8:30:** table time. Say the letter of the week and its sound, then do one page together, like a [letter tracing worksheet](/letter-tracing/). About 10 minutes.
- **8:45:** a number activity. Count snack crackers, or do one of the [pre-K math worksheets](/math-worksheets/pre-k/). About 5 minutes.
- **9:15:** snack, then outside play.
- **10:30:** story time. Two or three picture books on the couch.
- **11:00:** free play while you make lunch.
- **12:00:** lunch, then quiet time or a nap.
- **Afternoon:** art, music, errands, a playdate or more outside time.

That's 15 minutes of school in the whole day, and that's normal for preschool at home. Everything else is still learning: the talking at breakfast, the counting at snack, the books on the couch.

If your child naps in the morning or your mornings are busy, move table time to right after the nap. What matters is that it comes at the same point in the day.

## A weekly preschool homeschool schedule

A weekly rhythm keeps you from deciding everything each morning. Many families follow a letter of the week and a number of the week, with a simple pattern:

- **Monday:** meet the new letter. Say its sound, trace it, find it in a book.
- **Tuesday:** a letter hunt. Circle the letter on a page or look for it on signs and labels.
- **Wednesday:** art with the letter. Color a big letter, or make it from playdough.
- **Thursday:** number day. Count, trace and find the number of the week.
- **Friday:** no table work. A library trip, baking, a nature walk, or a review of anything your child liked.

Four short table days and one free day is a pace many families can keep up all year. If you miss a day, pick up where you left off. There is no catching up to do.

## How the schedule changes from 3 to 5

Kids grow a lot in these two years, so the same schedule won't fit all of them. As a rough guide:

- **Many 3 year olds:** 5 to 10 minutes of table time, mostly coloring, pointing and tracing with a finger. Lots of play and read-aloud.
- **Many 4 year olds:** 10 to 15 minutes. Tracing with a crayon or pencil, counting small groups, writing the first letter of their name.
- **Many 5 year olds (pre-K):** 15 to 20 minutes, sometimes split into two short sits. Writing their name, matching sounds to letters, counting to 20.

Every child develops at their own speed, and some days go better than others. Watch your child, not the clock. If they're still keen at the end, you can do one more page. If they're done after three minutes, that's a day's work too. If you have concerns, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

## A rhythm works better than a timetable

A clock schedule tells you what to do at 9:15. A rhythm tells you what comes after snack. For preschool at home, a rhythm is usually easier to keep, because it bends when breakfast runs late or the baby wakes up early.

Pick two or three anchors your day always has, like breakfast, lunch and bedtime, and hang everything else on them. "After breakfast we do letters. After snack we go outside. After lunch is quiet time." Your child learns the pattern within a week or two, and the arguments about what's next often get shorter.

A picture schedule helps a lot here. Preschoolers can't read a list, but they can look at a row of pictures and see that story time comes after outside. ## When the schedule falls apart

It will, and that's fine. A few things help:

- **Have a short version.** On a hard day, do one page or one book and call it done.
- **Keep the anchors, drop the rest.** If you only manage breakfast, letters and outside, the day still had a shape.
- **Use real life.** Count apples at the store, find letters on road signs, talk about the weather. A sick day on the couch with picture books still counts.
- **Change one thing at a time.** If mornings keep going wrong, try table time after snack for a week before changing anything else.

## Set up your first week in 20 minutes

1. **5 minutes:** pick your anchor. Choose the point in the day where table time goes, most likely right after breakfast.
2. **5 minutes:** make a picture schedule for the day with your child's name and print it. Let your child color it, then hang it at their eye level.
3. **5 minutes:** print the weekly chart and fill in this week's letter and number. Start with s and 1 if you have no plan yet.
4. **5 minutes:** print Monday's page, set out crayons, and put three picture books in a basket by the couch.
5. **Monday morning:** after breakfast, walk to the schedule together, point at the letters picture, and start.
