What are sight words? They are common words that kids learn to read at a glance, without stopping to sound them out. Words like "the," "said," "was" and "of" show up in almost every sentence, and several of them don't follow the usual phonics rules, so recognizing them instantly makes early reading much smoother.

Most schools work from one of two public-domain lists, Dolch or Fry. Below is how they differ, roughly how many words come up at each grade, and simple ways to practice at home.

## Why sight words matter for early readers

A beginning reader spends a lot of effort sounding out each word. If they also have to puzzle over "the" and "and" every time, the meaning of the sentence gets lost.

Knowing the most common words by sight frees up attention for the newer, harder words. It also helps with words that would trip up a child who is sounding things out. Try decoding "said," "one" or "who" letter by letter, and you get something quite different from the real word.

Sight words don't replace phonics. Kids still need to learn letter sounds and how to blend them. Think of sight words as the small set of everyday words that make simple books readable while phonics skills grow.

## Sight words vs high-frequency words

You'll hear both terms, often used to mean the same thing. Here is a simple way to tell them apart:

- **High-frequency words** are the words that appear most often in print. Some are easy to decode, like "and" or "it."
- **Sight words** are words a child recognizes instantly. In practice, that usually means the high-frequency words, especially the irregular ones like "said" or "come."

Some teachers call the irregular ones "heart words" or "tricky words," because part of the word has to be learned by heart. Your child may come home using any of these names.

## What are the Dolch sight words?

The Dolch list was compiled by Edward William Dolch from words that come up again and again in children's books. It was published in the 1930s and 1940s and is still common in classrooms today.

It has 220 "service words" (small words like "the," "because" and "which") plus a separate list of 95 nouns. The 220 words are grouped by level:

- **Pre-primer:** 40 words, such as a, and, big, can, come and see. Often used in preschool or early kindergarten. See the [preschool sight words list](/sight-words/pre-k/).
- **Primer:** 52 words, such as all, are, they, want, was and with. Many kindergarten classes use this set. See the [kindergarten sight words list](/sight-words/kindergarten/).
- **First grade:** 41 words, such as could, from, know, once and thank. See the [first grade sight words list](/sight-words/first-grade/).
- **Second grade:** 46 words, such as because, does, their and would.
- **Third grade:** 41 words, such as laugh, myself, together and shall.

Because it's short and sorted by grade, Dolch is an easy list to use at home. You can see every level, with a tracing worksheet and flash cards for each, on our [Dolch sight words lists by grade](/sight-words/) page.

## What are the Fry sight words?

The Fry list was put together by Edward Fry in the 1950s and updated in 1980. It ranks the 1,000 most common words in English reading material, in order of how often they appear.

Teachers usually work through Fry in groups of 100 words. The first 100 are the most common words of all, and they overlap heavily with the Dolch lists. Later groups bring in longer and less common words, so Fry stretches well past third grade.

Unlike Dolch, Fry is not sorted into grade levels by design. Schools that use it decide for themselves how many groups to cover each year.

## Dolch vs Fry: which list does your school use?

Both lists are built from the words kids meet most often in print, and the early words are much the same. The main differences:

- **Size.** Dolch has 220 service words plus 95 nouns. Fry has 1,000 words.
- **Order.** Dolch is grouped by grade level. Fry is ranked by how common each word is.
- **Reach.** Dolch covers pre-K to third grade. Fry continues into the upper elementary years.

Which list a school uses varies by district, by reading program, and sometimes by teacher. Some schools use their own list drawn from the books their curriculum uses. If you want home practice to match class work, ask your child's teacher which list they use and which words are coming up next. Many teachers are happy to send the list home.

## How many sight words per grade?

There's no single number, and goals vary a lot between schools. As a rough picture, if a school follows Dolch:

- Pre-K children may play with a handful of pre-primer words, often fewer than 20.
- Kindergarten classes often work through some or all of the pre-primer and primer lists, which together come to 92 words.
- First grade often adds the first grade list, and second and third grade finish the rest.

A school using Fry might aim for the first 100 words by the end of first grade, though this also varies. Kids move through these lists at very different speeds. If you have concerns about your child's reading, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

## How to practice sight words at home

Short and frequent beats long and rare. A few minutes most days works better than a long session once a week. Keep it light, and stop while your child is still enjoying it.

- **Start small.** Pick 3 or 4 words at a time. Add a new one only when the old ones come easily.
- **Read it, say it, trace it.** Have your child look at the word, say it out loud, then trace it. Writing the letters in order helps fix the spelling.
- **Find it in books.** When you read together, pause and let your child spot a practice word on the page.
- **Play quick games.** Lay out 5 flash cards, say a word, and see how fast your child can tap it. Or hide cards around a room for a word hunt.
- **Mix in known words.** Keep a few easy wins in every round so practice feels successful.

If a word keeps slipping, go back to tracing it and using it in a short spoken sentence ("I see a dog"). Some words, like "was" and "saw," get mixed up for a while. That's common in early readers.

## A 5-minute daily sight word routine

Here is a simple routine you can start tomorrow. You'll need flash cards for 4 words and a tracing sheet from the list your child is on.

1. **Warm up (1 minute).** Flip through the cards your child already knows. Read each one together, quickly.
2. **Meet the new word (1 minute).** Show one new card. Say it, point to each letter, and say it again. Ask your child to use it in a sentence out loud.
3. **Trace it (1 minute).** Trace the new word 3 times on the worksheet, saying the word each time.
4. **Tap game (1 minute).** Lay out all 4 cards. Say a word and let your child tap it. Swap roles so your child calls the word and you tap.
5. **Find it (1 minute).** Open a picture book to any page and hunt for one of the words together.

When all 4 words are easy for 3 days in a row, retire the oldest card to the review pile and bring in a new one.
