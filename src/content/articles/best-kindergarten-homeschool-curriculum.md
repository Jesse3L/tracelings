The best kindergarten homeschool curriculum is the one that teaches letter sounds and early reading in a clear order, keeps lessons short, and is easy for you to teach without a lot of prep. For most families that means a phonics-based plan, simple math with real objects, and about 15 to 30 minutes of table work a day. The fanciest box on the shelf is rarely the one a five year old needs.

Here's what to look for, what you can skip, and how to start this week without spending anything.

## What a kindergarten homeschool curriculum should cover

Kindergarten is mostly about three things: hearing and reading sounds, writing letters, and understanding numbers. A good plan covers each one a little bit at a time.

- **Letter sounds and phonics.** Kids learn what sound each letter makes, then blend sounds into short words like cat, pin and sun.
- **Sight words.** A small set of common words that are hard to sound out, like the, said and was. Many plans use the Dolch pre-primer and primer lists.
- **Handwriting.** Forming capital and lowercase letters, then writing their name and short words on lined paper.
- **Early math.** Counting to 20 and beyond, recognizing numbers, comparing more and less, and adding within 5 or 10.
- **Read-aloud time.** Picture books read together every day. This is where vocabulary and a love of stories grow, and no worksheet replaces it.

Science, art, music and social studies are lovely extras at this age. Nature walks, kitchen experiments and library books cover a lot of ground without a formal program.

## Phonics first: why order matters

Look closely at the order a curriculum teaches letters in. Plans that start with a few useful sounds (often s, a, t, i, p and n) let kids read real words within weeks. That early success keeps them interested.

After single letters, many programs move to word families. A word family is a group of words with the same ending, like -at in hat, mat and sat. Once a child reads -at, swapping the first letter gives them a whole set of new words. ## Signs a curriculum is a good fit

Every family is different, but a few things tend to make kindergarten go smoothly.

- **Short lessons.** Five year olds often focus well for 10 to 20 minutes at a time. A plan that expects an hour of seat work will likely end in frustration.
- **Open and go.** You can pick it up and teach without hours of prep. A simple daily plan beats a thick teacher's manual you never open.
- **Hands-on parts.** Counting snacks, building words with magnetic letters, tracing letters in salt. Kids this age learn through their hands.
- **Room to repeat.** You can slow down or repeat a week without falling "behind."
- **A price that fits.** There are good free and low-cost options. Spending more does not guarantee a better year.

## All-in-one boxes vs. putting it together yourself

Curricula usually come in two styles.

An **all-in-one** program bundles reading, math and other subjects into one daily schedule. It saves planning time and works well if you want everything decided for you. The tradeoff is cost and less flexibility.

A **mix-and-match** approach picks one resource per subject, for example a phonics plan, a math book and library books. It costs less and fits your child better, but you do more of the planning.

Many families land in the middle: a simple core plan for reading and math, plus books and play for everything else.

## What you can skip in kindergarten

You don't need everything a catalog shows you.

- **Long worksheet packets.** A page or two a day is plenty. More paper doesn't mean more learning.
- **Grades and tests.** Watching your child read a word or count a pile of blocks tells you what you need to know.
- **Screens for core skills.** Apps can be fun extras, but letter sounds and early math stick best with a grown-up beside them.
- **A strict school-hours schedule.** Kindergarten at home often fits into a morning, with the rest of the day for play.

## How to check your state's homeschool rules

Homeschool laws vary a lot from state to state. Some states ask you to file a notice, keep records or cover certain subjects, and others ask for very little. Check your state education department's website or a statewide homeschool group before you start, and keep a simple record of what you cover each week. Dated plan pages and a folder of finished work are easy ways to do that.

## When to adjust the pace

Some kids are reading short books by the end of kindergarten, and some are still learning letter sounds. Both are common. Slow down if lessons bring tears, and move faster if your child is bored and already knows the material.

Many kids reverse letters like b and d at this age, and that is usually part of learning to write. If you have concerns about your child's reading, writing or attention, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

## Try this tomorrow morning

You can start a kindergarten homeschool routine tomorrow with a printer and a box of crayons. Here's a simple 20-minute start.

1. **Read aloud (5 minutes).** Pick a picture book and read it together. Point to one word you see often, like the or and.
2. **Letter sounds (5 minutes).** Print week 2 of the free [kindergarten plan](/homeschool/kindergarten/) and trace S and A together, saying the sounds /s/ and /a/ each time.
3. **Sight words (5 minutes).** Trace two words from the [kindergarten sight words](/sight-words/kindergarten/) list and look for them in your book.
4. **Math (5 minutes).** Count out 5 crackers, eat 2, and count what's left. Then trace a number on a [number tracing worksheet](/number-tracing/1-20/).
5. **Stop while it's fun.** End on a win, even if you didn't finish the page.

Do this four or five mornings a week, and add a new week each Monday. If your child isn't ready for kindergarten work yet, the free [preschool homeschool curriculum](/homeschool/preschool/) uses the same routine with one letter a week.
