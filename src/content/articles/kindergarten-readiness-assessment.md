A kindergarten readiness assessment is usually a short, friendly screening where a teacher watches a child do a few simple things: say their name, name some letters, count objects, copy a shape and follow a direction. It is meant to give the school a picture of where each child is starting, not to pass or fail anyone. What it covers varies by district, so the best first step is to ask your school.

If you know what the tasks tend to look like, you can practice them in small, relaxed ways at home. Here is what many screenings include, how to prepare without drilling, and what to do if you have questions.

## What a kindergarten readiness assessment usually looks like

Many schools use a one-on-one meeting. A teacher or aide sits with your child at a small table, often with picture cards, blocks and a pencil or crayon. The whole thing may feel like a game to your child.

Other schools watch a small group at play, or send home a short parent questionnaire. Some districts do not screen before the first day and instead assess in the first weeks of school.

The results are usually used to plan classroom groups and to notice kids who may benefit from extra help. They are a snapshot of one day, and a child who is tired, shy or hungry can look different on another day.

## Tasks your child might be asked to do

Every district chooses its own tools, so treat this as a list of common examples and not a guarantee. Many screenings touch on a handful of these:

- **Say their first and last name,** and sometimes their age
- **Write their name,** with any letters they can manage
- **Name letters,** often shown in a mixed-up order
- **Count objects,** such as five blocks, touching each one once
- **Copy shapes,** like a circle, a cross or a square
- **Follow a direction,** such as "Put the card under the block"
- **Name colors and shapes**
- **Retell or answer questions about a short story or picture**
- **Cut with safety scissors or build with small pieces**

Some screenings also ask about numbers, such as naming a numeral or saying what comes next. A few include simple word recognition. Most of these tasks are about seeing what a child already does, and a lot of children will not do every item.

## Why assessments vary by district

There is no single national kindergarten screening. States, districts and even individual schools pick their own tools and timing. Entry age cutoffs and registration steps vary too.

That is why a friend's experience in another town may not match yours. To find out what your child will face, call the school office or check the district enrollment page. You can ask:

- Is there a screening, and when is it?
- What does it include, and how long does it take?
- How are the results used?
- Can a parent stay in the room?

One phone call often takes away most of the worry.

## How to practice without drilling

Short, playful practice works better than long sessions. Five to ten minutes is plenty, and skipping a day is fine. Keep it light, and stop when your child loses interest.

Name writing is the task parents ask about most. A [free name tracing worksheet](/name-tracing/) with your child's actual name shows where each letter starts and how big to make it. Use a capital first letter and lowercase after that, so the habit matches what most classrooms teach. Many children do this somewhere between 4 and 5, and wobbly or oversized letters are normal.

For letters, start with the ones your child sees most, like the ones in their name. A few minutes of [letter tracing worksheets](/letter-tracing/) a couple of times a week is enough. Mix in pointing out letters on cereal boxes and street signs, since screenings often show letters out of order.

For counting, daily life does most of the work: stairs, grapes, forks on the table. If your child likes paper practice, [number tracing pages for 1 to 20](/number-tracing/1-20/) give a clear next step once counting objects feels easy. Pair each number with a small pile of things so the numeral means something.

If your child is curious about short words like "the" and "and," a [pre-K sight words list](/sight-words/pre-k/) gives a small, sensible set to play with. If not, skip it. Reading aloud together every day is worth more than flash cards.

For a wider look at self-care, social skills and fine motor skills, see the [kindergarten readiness checklist](/learn/kindergarten-readiness-checklist/).

## Keeping the day calm

Your mood travels. If you treat the screening like a fun visit, your child is more likely to as well.

- **Talk about it ahead of time.** Say that a teacher will ask some questions and look at some pictures, and that there are no wrong answers.
- **Plan a rested morning.** Aim for a normal sleep schedule and a real breakfast.
- **Skip the cramming.** A quick review in the car can make a child anxious.
- **Pack a small comfort.** A familiar routine afterward, like a snack or a stop at the park, gives the day a happy ending.

If your child freezes or says "I don't know," that is useful information for the teacher too. Many kids warm up as the visit goes on.

## What the results do and don't tell you

A screening shows a few skills on one day. It does not measure how curious or kind a child is, how well they will learn to read, or how their year will go. Children in the same class often start with very different letter and number knowledge, and teachers plan for that.

Ask the school how the results will be shared. If something stands out, a teacher can often explain what it means and how to support it at home. If you have concerns about your child's speech, hearing, attention or hand skills, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician. They can suggest next steps, including a referral to a specialist when it makes sense.

## A simple 10-minute practice plan for the week before

Use this any time in the weeks before a screening, three or four days a week. Keep it playful and skip any day that is not working.

1. **Name practice, 3 minutes.** Trace the name once on a name tracing page, then write it once on a blank line. Praise the effort, not the neatness.
2. **Letter hunt, 2 minutes.** Write five letters on sticky notes, including the first letter of your child's name. Hide them in a room and ask your child to bring you one at a time while you say its name.
3. **Count and match, 3 minutes.** Choose a number from 1 to 10. Your child counts out that many crackers, then points to the numeral on a tracing page.
4. **Shape and direction game, 2 minutes.** Draw a circle and a cross on paper for your child to copy. Then give two-step directions like "Put the block on the chair, then clap once."

Date the name sample each week and keep it in a folder. A few weeks of samples give you a calm, honest picture of where your child is starting.
