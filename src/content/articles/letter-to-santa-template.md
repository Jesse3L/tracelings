The best letter to Santa template for a young child matches how they write right now, not how a third grader writes. For many 3 year olds that means drawing a picture while you write down their words. Many 4 year olds can trace a short dotted letter, and many 5 to 7 year olds can fill in a few blanks on their own, with every version ending in a name they sign themselves.

Below are three template setups you can make at home in about 10 minutes, the wording to use for each, and a calm way to spread the writing over a few days.

## What goes in a simple letter to Santa template

Keep the letter short. One page, five lines at most, and big spaces between them. A young child who sees a full page of lines often gives up before they start.

A good template has four parts:

- **A greeting:** "Dear Santa,"
- **One line about them:** their age, or something they did this year
- **One or two wishes:** a toy, a book, or something for someone else
- **A closing and signature:** "Love," and their name

That's all. You can add a box at the top for a drawing, which gives early writers a way to say more than they can spell.

## Supplies you'll need

- Plain white paper or cardstock
- A highlighter or a light yellow marker for writing dotted or light letters
- Chunky crayons, a thick pencil, or washable markers
- A printed name tracing sheet (optional, for signature practice)
- An envelope and a few stickers

If you'd rather print than hand-write the dotted lines, our [free word tracing worksheet maker](/word-tracing/) lets you type "Dear Santa" or a full sentence and print it as dotted letters on handwriting lines. Print it, then glue a drawing box above it.

## For 3 year olds: draw and dictate

Many 3 year olds are still working on lines, circles, and holding a crayon. Writing whole words isn't the goal yet. Instead, your child makes the pictures and you do the writing.

1. Fold a sheet of paper in half. The top half is for the drawing.
2. Ask, "What would you like to tell Santa?" Write their answer word for word on the bottom half.
3. Your child draws the toy, their family, or anything they like in the top half. Scribbles count.
4. At the bottom, write "Love," then the first letter of their name in highlighter.
5. Your child traces that one letter.

Writing down exactly what they say, even when it's silly, shows them that their words can go on paper. That idea comes before handwriting.

## For 4 year olds: trace the words

Many 4 year olds enjoy tracing and can follow a dotted line for a short word or two. Some can trace their whole name. Letters may wobble, flip, or drift above the line, and that's very common.

Set up the page like this:

1. At the top, write "Dear Santa," in dotted letters.
2. Below it, write one short wish line in dotted letters: "I want a ___." Leave the blank empty.
3. Your child traces the dotted words.
4. For the blank, they draw the toy or glue on a picture cut from a catalog.
5. At the bottom, write "Love," in dotted letters, then their name.

Keep the traced words to about six. If your child's name has a lot of letters, count it toward that total.

The S in "Santa" is a hard letter for a lot of young writers, because it curves two ways. If it keeps coming out backward, a few minutes on a [letter S tracing page](/letter-tracing/s/) beforehand gives them a warm-up. You can also write a big S on paper and let them trace it with a finger first.

## For 5 to 7 year olds: fill in the blanks

Many kids in this range can write some words on their own, often using sounded-out spelling like "dol" for "doll" or "skatbord" for "skateboard." That kind of spelling is a normal part of learning to write, so let it stand on the letter.

A fill-in template gives structure without a blank page:

- Dear Santa,
- My name is ________.
- I am ____ years old.
- This year I learned how to ________.
- I would like ________.
- I hope you bring ________ something too.
- Love, ________

Write the starter words in regular pencil or print them, and leave the blanks on handwriting lines. Your child fills in each blank. For a 5 year old, three blanks may be plenty. A 7 year old might fill all of them and add a sentence of their own at the bottom.

The "something for someone else" line is a nice touch. It gives a child one more thing to write that isn't about toys.

## End with a traced signature

Every version ends the same way: your child signs their own name. A signature gives the letter a real feeling, and kids often take extra care when it's going to someone important.

To make it easy:

- **Print a name sheet first.** A [free name tracing worksheet](/name-tracing/) with your child's name lets them practice the signature on scrap before the real letter.
- **Write it dotted on the letter.** Use highlighter so the dotted name is light and easy to trace over.
- **Use a capital first letter, then lowercase.** "Maya," not "MAYA." It matches what many schools teach.
- **For confident writers,** skip the dots and let them sign on their own, with the traced sheet next to them as a model.

Kids develop at different speeds. Some 4 year olds write their name without help, and some 6 year olds still like a dotted line. If you have concerns about your child's grip or writing, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician.

## Tips for teachers making a class set

A Santa letter works well as a December writing center, with a few changes for a group:

- **Print two or three versions.** Put draw-and-dictate, trace, and fill-in templates in separate trays so each child picks the one that fits.
- **Prep names ahead.** Print a name tracing sheet for each child so signatures don't hold up the line.
- **Offer a no-Santa option.** Not every family celebrates Christmas or does Santa. A "Dear Friend" or "Dear Grandma" letter with the same structure works just as well, and nobody is left out.
- **Keep a quiet task nearby.** Children who finish early can color one of our [Christmas coloring pages](/coloring-pages/christmas-coloring-pages/) to fold into their envelope.

## Ideas for the drawing box

Some kids freeze when they're asked to draw "anything." Offer one of these ideas instead:

- The toy they hope for most
- Their family by the tree
- A snack to leave out for Santa
- Something kind they did this year

You can also let your child cut pictures from a toy catalog and glue them in the box. Cutting and gluing are good hand practice, and it's a big help for kids who don't like to draw.

## A three-day Santa letter plan

Writing a whole letter in one sitting is a lot for small hands. About 10 minutes a day over three days keeps it fun:

1. **Day 1 (10 minutes):** Talk about what to say. Your child draws in the picture box while you jot down their ideas.
2. **Day 2 (10 minutes):** Your child traces or fills in "Dear Santa" and the wish line. Stop after two or three lines, even if they're going strong.
3. **Day 3, warm-up (3 minutes):** Your child traces their name once on a name tracing sheet.
4. **Day 3, finish (7 minutes):** They trace or write "Love," sign the letter, fold it, and decorate the envelope with stickers.
5. **Afterward:** Write the date and your child's age on the back, then snap a photo or tuck a copy away.

Next December, set last year's letter next to the new one. The change in a single name, from one traced letter to a signature, is one of the easiest ways to see how much your child has grown.
