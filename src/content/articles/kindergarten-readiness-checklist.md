A good kindergarten readiness checklist focuses on independence and getting along with others first, then on early letters and numbers. Many kids starting kindergarten can use the bathroom on their own, follow a two-step direction, recognize their name, and hold a crayon well enough to draw. Reading, writing full sentences, and counting high are things kindergarten itself is there to teach.

Below is a checklist grouped by area, with notes on which items are worth practicing now and which ones you can relax about. Schools and districts differ, so use it as a guide, not a test.

## How to use this kindergarten readiness checklist

Read through each group and put a mark next to the things your child does most days. Don't aim for every box. A child who has most of the self-care and social items is usually in good shape, even if the letters section is still patchy.

Each section ends with a note on what kids often pick up during the kindergarten year. Those items are nice to have, not entry tickets.

If you want to know exactly what your school looks at, call the front office or check the district website. Many schools share a short list at registration or run a screening in the spring or summer.

## Self-care skills

These skills make the first weeks of school much easier. One teacher with a full class can't zip every coat.

- **Bathroom.** Uses the toilet, wipes, flushes and washes hands with little help.
- **Clothes.** Puts on a coat and shoes. Velcro shoes are fine. Tying laces is not expected.
- **Lunch.** Opens a lunchbox, most containers and a yogurt tube. Practice with the actual lunchbox at home.
- **Belongings.** Puts things in a backpack and takes them out again.
- **Asking for help.** Tells an adult when they're hurt, sick or need the bathroom.

Often learned in kindergarten: zipping a coat alone, opening tricky packages, and keeping track of a folder.

## Social and emotional skills

Kindergarten is a big room full of new people. Kids don't need to be calm all the time, but some practice with these helps.

- Separates from a parent, even if there are a few tears at first
- Takes turns in a simple game with some reminders
- Sits and listens to a short picture book
- Follows a two-step direction, like "Put your cup away and grab your shoes"
- Uses words for big feelings at least some of the time

Often learned in kindergarten: waiting longer, raising a hand, working in a small group, and solving small conflicts with a friend.

## Fine motor skills

Fine motor skills are the small movements of the hands and fingers. They are what make holding a pencil, cutting and gluing possible.

- Holds a crayon with the fingers, not a whole fist (the grip doesn't need to be perfect)
- Copies a straight line, a circle and a cross
- Snips paper with safety scissors
- Uses a glue stick and peels a sticker
- Builds with small blocks or snaps pieces together

Short crayons, play dough, and picking up small things with tongs all build hand strength. Coloring and tracing build control. If your child is still holding a crayon in a fist most of the time, a few weeks of play dough and torn-paper collages often help more than worksheets.

Often learned in kindergarten: cutting along a curved line, copying a triangle, and writing letters at a steady size.

## Letters and name writing

This is the section parents worry about most. Many kids do a few of these before school and pick up the rest in the first months.

- Recognizes their own name in print
- Writes their first name, even if the letters are big, wobbly or out of order
- Names some letters, especially the ones in their name
- Knows that print goes left to right and that words carry meaning
- Enjoys being read to

Name writing is the one many schools ask about. A [free name tracing worksheet](/name-tracing/) with your child's actual name lets them practice where each letter starts. Use a capital first letter and lowercase after that ("Leo," not "LEO") so they don't have to relearn it in class. If you want more on what to expect by age, see [can 4 year olds write their name](/learn/can-4-year-olds-write-their-name/).

For letters beyond the name, a few minutes with [alphabet letter tracing worksheets](/letter-tracing/) a couple of times a week is plenty. Start with letters your child sees often.

Some kids are curious about short words like "the" and "and." If yours is, a [pre-K sight words list](/sight-words/pre-k/) gives you a small, sensible set to play with. If not, skip it for now.

Often learned in kindergarten: naming every letter, matching letters to sounds, reading simple words, and writing a short sentence.

## Numbers and early math

Early math at this age is more about understanding amounts than reciting.

- Counts out loud to 10, and maybe beyond
- Counts a small pile of objects, touching each one once
- Recognizes some numerals, like 1 to 5
- Sorts things by color, size or shape
- Names a circle, a square and a triangle

Everyday counting covers most of this: stairs, snacks, forks on the table. If your child likes paper practice, [number tracing worksheets for 1 to 10](/number-tracing/1-10/) are a good size for one sitting. Pair each number with a small pile of objects so the numeral means something.

Often learned in kindergarten: counting to 100, writing numbers to 20, and simple adding and taking away with objects.

## What schools commonly look at

Readiness screenings vary a lot by district and state. Some schools don't screen at all. Others do a short one-on-one meeting where a teacher might ask a child to say their name, name some letters, count objects, copy a shape, or follow a simple direction.

These screenings are usually used to plan support and group kids, not to keep them out. If your district runs one, ask what it covers and how the results are used. That one phone call often removes most of the stress.

Kindergarten entry rules (age cutoffs, required forms, vaccinations) also differ by state and district. Your district's enrollment page is the best place to check.

## When to talk to someone

Kids grow at different speeds, and a lot can change between spring and the first day of school. A summer birthday child and a fall birthday child can look quite different at registration, and both can be ready.

It can still help to ask for input if your child has a hard time with many items in more than one section, if they avoid drawing and play with small objects entirely, or if you just have a nagging feeling. If you have concerns, ask your child's teacher or pediatrician. A preschool teacher sees many kids this age and can give a useful comparison. For hand and grip questions, a pediatrician may suggest an occupational therapist.

## A simple 15-minute readiness routine

Try this three or four days a week over the summer, or any time in the months before school starts. Skip a day whenever it isn't working.

1. **Self-care practice, 3 minutes.** Put on shoes and a coat without help, or open every container in tomorrow's lunch. Use a timer and make it a race against the clock, not against a sibling.
2. **Name hunt, 2 minutes.** Write your child's name and two others on sticky notes. Hide them around a room and have your child bring back only theirs.
3. **Trace and write, 4 minutes.** One line of name tracing. Then one try writing it alone under the traced line.
4. **Count and match, 3 minutes.** Pick a number from 1 to 10. Your child counts out that many crackers or blocks and finds the numeral on the page.
5. **Read together, 3 minutes.** One short picture book. Pause once to ask what might happen next.

Date one name sample each week and keep them in a folder. By the first day of school you'll have a small stack that shows real progress, and a calmer picture of where your child stands.
